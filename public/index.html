§ 02

Opened up

Client names and confidential terms are missing on purpose. That firewall is part of the design, not an omission.

JD Package Engine

Recruiting ops
Problem

Every search started from a blank page. Intake calls produced an hour of messy transcript, and the resulting job descriptions drifted in voice and structure depending on who wrote them. Sensitive client detail leaked into candidate-facing copy.

Built

A pipeline that turns one transcript into a full package:

  • Published JD, third person, website-ready
  • Internal brief with verbatim calibration quotes
  • Outreach #1 and #2, to a strict word budget
  • Intro email, booking link front-loaded
  • Platform fields and screening questions
  • Green-flag / red-flag criteria per role
Rules that hold it
  • No client name or URL before the intro-email stage. Outreach uses stage, backer, category and traction signal instead
  • Off-record intel and restricted commercial terms live only in the internal brief, with distribution flagged
  • Anything demographic gets marked non-actionable and never shapes sourcing
  • Conflicting sources get reconciled and flagged, never silently resolved
Result

Around 200 job descriptions on this template, adopted org-wide. Turnaround dropped from a from-scratch write to an edit pass. Nothing confidential has to be caught by a reviewer, because it never enters the published doc at all.

Inbox Tracker

Pipeline hygiene
Problem

Intro emails go out from a shared team address; replies land in mine. Across dozens of concurrent searches, someone who replies on day four and hears nothing is a lost placement. Manual scanning missed people and ate the first hour of every morning.

Built

A daily scan with defined queries and a fixed classification model. Every thread lands in exactly one bucket, and anyone past the three-day threshold gets a follow-up draft written automatically.

How it runs
  • Three queries cover the window: today's inbox, sent intros, threads I'm on
  • A reply only counts from a non-team address that isn't an auto-reply
  • Day three with no answer flips a thread into the queue
  • Drafts are created, never sent. I review before anything goes
Result

Triage went from an open-ended morning task to a report with a defined output. Nobody sits unanswered, and the follow-up decision is a rule rather than a judgment call I re-make every day.

Daily Call Brief

Executive support
Problem

Leadership walks into back-to-back client calls across 50+ accounts. The context for each one, from where the search stands to what was promised last time and who is waiting on us, was scattered across calendar, Slack and the tracker.

Built

A brief assembled every morning: today's calls in order, and for each the open roles, pipeline movement since last contact, outstanding commitments, and the one thing that most needs a decision.

How it runs
  • Calendar sets the order and the deadline
  • Slack supplies what changed since last contact
  • Pipeline data supplies stage counts and stalls
  • Anything unresolved is listed as an open item, not omitted
Result

Calls start with context instead of reconstruction. Questions that used to surface mid-call get answered before it. The format is fixed, so it reads in under two minutes.

Outreach Kit

Templates
Constraint

Outreach fails long. Every message has a word budget and a fixed beat structure, so writing one is assembly rather than composition.

Four touchpoints
  • Outreach #1: three paragraphs, about 120 words
  • Outreach #2: one sentence, about 40 words
  • Intro email: about 200 words, link in the first two lines
  • Follow-up: triggered by the tracker, not by memory
Banned
  • Company background walls and full benefits lists
  • Interview process breakdowns before a recruiter call
  • Repeated visa and relocation disclaimers
  • False personalization: no "saw your profile" unless it's true
Why it's a system

Because the rules are written down, anyone can produce outreach that reads consistently, and a reviewer checks it against a list instead of a feeling.

Payroll & Onboarding Runbooks

Back office
Problem

Payroll across Colombia and international contractors meant different compliance rules, currencies and rails on one monthly deadline. Onboarding lived in the head of whoever ran the last one, so every hire cost a senior person a week.

Built

Written runbooks. The payroll cycle as a dated checklist with verification before each run, and onboarding as a sequence covering contracts, accounts, tooling and first-week training, with every step naming its owner.

Also standardized
  • Client intake, contracts and follow-up sequences
  • Insurance eligibility checks before services start
  • Referral review with document requests tracked to close
  • QA passes on internal tools, bugs routed to engineering
Result

Payroll runs on schedule and survives a handoff. Documented onboarding halved new-hire ramp time in a prior role. The procedure is the training material.

Contact blockTwo columns
Canva: name 23 pt semibold, letter spacing −30. Title 9 pt regular. Rule 0.5 pt. Contact 8 pt, line spacing 1.45.
The name is past the safe margin. Size it down, or the long-name agents get a broken card.
Brief

A card layout that has to hold for eighty-five people. One card is not the problem. The template is, and it always breaks on the name.

Constraints
  • No brand guide, so weight and size carry the hierarchy instead of colour
  • Six pieces of contact information have to fit without crowding the name
  • Nothing within a quarter inch of the trim
What I decided
  • The person's name is the largest element, not the brokerage. A card is handed over by a human.
  • Name and title pair tightly, then a rule opens space before the contact block, so the eye reads two groups instead of five lines
  • Contact runs in two columns rather than one stack, which stops the lower third going heavy
  • Tracking pulled in slightly. At this size the default spacing reads loose against the small text under it.

Canva: mark 2.1 in, name 1.6 in, phone 2.0 in, rider 1.5 in. Margins 2 in all round.
That is wider than the sign. It will spill past the 2 in margin.
Brief

Signage that stays legible from across a street, in the rain, at thirty kilometres an hour. The only piece here where viewing distance sets the type size instead of taste.

Constraints
  • Roughly one inch of cap height per ten feet of reading distance, so the phone number sets its own minimum
  • Three pieces of information at most. Anything past that will not be read.
  • The rider is one line, with no room to be clever
What I decided
  • Brokerage largest, at the top. From the street the brand is what identifies the sign; the agent details are for people who already stopped.
  • Contrast over colour. A mid tone on a mid tone disappears at distance even when it looks fine on screen.
  • Generous margins, because signs get bent, mounted crooked and half hidden by a hedge.
  • Use the step-back control rather than your eye at full screen. That is the only honest test.

At this value the text is fighting the bright photo on the left. Check the first tile, not the last.
Brief

New listing, open house, sold. Three posts that read as a set when they surface months apart in a feed, and that someone else can fill in without asking me anything.

Constraints
  • Photos arrive at unpredictable crops and quality, so the layout cannot depend on the image
  • Text has to stay legible over photography without an overlay swallowing the house
  • Posted weekly, so filling it in should take minutes
What I decided
  • The text block holds the same position on all three. Only the label and the photo change, which is what makes them read as a series.
  • A gradient scrim instead of a flat box, so the photo stays visible and one value works over both bright and dark images
  • The address is the headline, not the status. People scroll for the property.

Document design

Contrario AI · adopted org-wide · around 200 documents built on it

Role specification
Founding Engineer
Series A · agentic payments · San Francisco
About the role

You would own the ledger and settlement engine end to end, from the data model through the reconciliation logic that finance actually closes the month on.

What you will own
Design the double-entry ledger and its invariants
Ship the settlement engine to production
Decide the build and buy line on payment rails
Must have

Five years building systems where money moves, and the judgment to know which correctness problems are worth slowing down for.

Rebuilt here from the original, which lives in a client account.

Real prior work, rebuilt
Brief

Every recruiter wrote job descriptions in their own format, so clients received documents that did not look like they came from the same company. One template the team would actually use.

Constraints
  • Documents get pasted into LinkedIn, Notion and client email, so the hierarchy has to survive losing all of its styling
  • Non-designers fill them in daily, so anything fussy is broken within a week
  • Existing colours and one typeface, with no licensing budget
What I decided
  • Three levels of hierarchy and no more, which are role title, section and body, sized far enough apart to read the structure at a glance
  • Section headings carry weight rather than colour, so the page survives greyscale printing and a plain-text paste
  • A fixed line length on body copy. That was the change that made the documents start to feel finished.
Result

Around 200 job descriptions built on it, and the JD Package Engine in section 02 is this template with the writing rules attached.

§ 06
How I got here

    

      
      

        
I'm from Córdoba, on the Colombian Caribbean coast. My first real job was at the town hall in Chinú, where I administered a database of 8,000+ families whose subsidies depended on their kids staying in school and up to date on health checks. Fifty people a day came to the counter. If a record was wrong, a family didn't eat that month.

        
That's where the whole thing starts. I studied Systems and Telecommunications Engineering, taught adults Windows and Office at SENA, then spent two years in Boston as an au pair, which is how I actually learned English, and how I learned that a schedule for three children under five is a production system with no margin.

      

      

        
Everything since has been the same job in a different costume: make the process survive the person running it.

        
Since then it's been remote operations for US teams: executive support in Miami, a back office in Colombia, then payroll, intake and SOPs at a YC health startup, and now recruiting operations for another one, across 50+ active clients.

        
The pattern is consistent enough that I stopped pretending it was a coincidence. I get handed something repetitive and undocumented, and I hand back a written procedure that somebody else can run. Then I go find the next one.
